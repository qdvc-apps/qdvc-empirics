## 7. LLM response

### Methodological commentary

**The overall move fits the literature well.** Juxtaposing theory-led and data-led coding of the same text is how several established approaches work:
- hybrid inductive–deductive thematic analysis (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006);
- template analysis (King), where a priori themes are revised against the data;
- directed content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005);
- abductive analysis (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012; 2014), which is about noticing where data surprise existing theory.

Abduction isn't a third kind of coding. It happens when the two kinds are put side by side. So making it a view across layers, rather than a third layer type, is the right call.

**Some items under "Deductive" aren't deductive.** Maxwell (2013) separates three kinds of category: organizational (topics fixed in advance, such as interview questions), substantive and theoretical. Applied to your list:
- **Protocols** are organizational. Applying them is what Saldaña calls *structural coding* (from MacQueen et al., 2008): it tags where a topic or question sits, not what it means.
- **Interviewees and Tagsheets** are descriptive case data. Saldaña calls this *attribute coding*, and NVivo calls it classifications. Tagsheets also apply to open and axial codes, which sit under "Inductive", so placing them under "Deductive" pulls against that.
- Only **A Priori Codes** are deductive in the strict sense.

One alternative grouping:

```
Project      Empirical Files, Participants, Researchers, Layers, Project Settings
Structure    Protocols, Tagsheets (or Attribute Sheets)
Deductive    A Priori Codes
Inductive    Open Codes, Axial Codes
```

Your grouping still works as a practical "defined beforehand vs emerging" split. If you keep it, I'd at least note in the docs that "Deductive" there means "predefined".

**Names**
- **Interviewees → Participants.** Your own example of research articles as files shows interviews aren't the only data. Focus groups, observation notes and documents all have people who aren't interviewees.
- **Tagsheets.** You're right that "codebook" means something else. In MacQueen et al. (1998) and Boyatzis (1998), a codebook is the set of code definitions, which is your new A Priori Codes. "Tag" is often used to mean a code, though, so "Tagsheet" could confuse. The established terms are *attribute sheets*, *descriptors* (Dedoose) or *classifications* (NVivo). "Attribute Sheets" (`AS 1`) matches Saldaña's term.
- **Bring "Codebook" back for the A Priori Codes list,** or for a generated export of it. That is exactly what a codebook is.
- **A Priori Codes.** Widely understood. Saldaña's *provisional codes* (Miles & Huberman's "start list") stresses that they're expected to change, which suits abduction. Either name is defensible.
- **Deductive / Inductive.** Strictly, applying predefined codes is theory-*guided*, not logical deduction. Gibbs (2007) and Kuckartz say *concept-driven* and *data-driven* for that reason. I'd keep Deductive/Inductive because researchers recognise them, perhaps with those phrases as subtitles.
- **Axial codes** are specific to Strauss & Corbin. Glaser rejects them, and Charmaz uses *focused coding*. That's fine; just note the lineage in the docs.

**Corrections to the a priori code fields.** The standard codebook entries (MacQueen et al., 1998; Boyatzis, 1998) have:
- name;
- brief definition and full definition;
- when to use (your "What is included?");
- when not to use (your "What is excluded?");
- example (your "Ideal example").

Your list is missing the **definition**, which is the field coders rely on most. Two more are worth adding:
- **Counter-example (near miss):** text that looks as if it fits but doesn't. Boyatzis includes negative examples, and they matter most for consistency between coders.
- **Source:** the theory or literature the code comes from. This keeps deductive codes traceable and is useful in a methods section.

**Protocols vs Tagsheets.** Both are "predefined questions and answers", so the docs should draw the line clearly:
- A protocol marks **where in the text** something occurs.
- A tagsheet records **what the researcher concludes about** an item.

**Your research-article example** opens up research synthesis. In meta-ethnography (Noblit & Hare, 1988) and thematic synthesis (Thomas & Harden, 2008), *first-order constructs* (participants' words), *second-order constructs* (the authors' interpretations) and *third-order constructs* (the reviewer's) are kept apart. A protocol along those lines would make the app useful for literature synthesis as well as primary data.

**Shared or per-layer definitions.** This matters most for the data model. A priori codes are a shared instrument: two coders apply the *same* codebook. If they're defined per layer, as open codes are now, two deductive layers can't be compared code for code. I'd make Protocols and A Priori Codes project-wide, with only the selections stored per layer. Open and axial codes can stay per layer, since emergent codes belong to a coder's reading.

**Features that would directly support abduction**
- **Links between inductive and deductive codes,** with types such as *confirms*, *refines*, *extends*, *contradicts* and *not covered by theory*. This extends the existing interrelations and is where abductive "surprises" get recorded.
- **Overlap matrix:** a priori codes against open/axial codes, counting where their selections overlap in the text. It shows where theory and data meet, and where they don't.
- **Promote and revise:** turn an open code into an a priori code, or revise an a priori code, keeping a "derived from" trail. That is how template analysis works.
- **Coverage report for protocols:** which protocol items occur in which files. This shows interview fidelity, or which article sections are missing.
- **Suggested structural segments:** match the wording of protocol questions to interviewer turns (the speaker detection already exists), or match headings in Markdown, to propose protocol segments for confirmation.
- **Agreement between two deductive layers:** percent agreement or Cohen's κ, possible once codes are shared. It should be optional and clearly labelled. It suits codebook approaches (O'Connor & Joffe, 2020) but not reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke).
- **Later:** a framework matrix (participants or files against a priori codes, Ritchie & Spencer; Gale et al., 2013), analytic memos as their own items, and a disconfirming-case flag on selections.
